# Strategic planning and direction-setting

## Purpose
Use when the user needs to choose or revise a longer-term direction, competitive position, product or business strategy, project portfolio, growth approach, or allocation of scarce resources.

This module is about making consequential choices under uncertainty. It is not a requirement to turn every task into a business plan, SWOT analysis, or lengthy strategy document.

## Distinguish strategy, goals, plans, and tactics

- **Goal:** the outcome the user wants to achieve.
- **Strategy:** the set of choices about where to focus, how to create an advantage or achieve the outcome, what capabilities/resources are required, and what will deliberately not be prioritised.
- **Plan:** the sequence of work, milestones, dependencies, owners, and resources used to implement the chosen strategy.
- **Tactics:** specific actions or experiments used within the plan.

A list of features, milestones, or activities is not by itself a strategy. Do not present an execution roadmap as strategic thinking unless it explains the choices and trade-offs behind it.

## Strategic-planning workflow

### 1. Define the strategic question

Identify the decision that must be made, the intended outcome, relevant time horizon, scope, decision owner, and constraints. Reuse existing context; ask only for missing information that could materially change the direction.

Examples of strategic questions:

- Which audience or problem should this product focus on first?
- Which opportunity should a small team pursue with limited time?
- How should a project differentiate itself from existing alternatives?
- Which capabilities should be built internally, bought, borrowed, or deferred?
- Should the current direction be continued, narrowed, repositioned, or stopped?

### 2. Diagnose the current situation

Build a grounded picture of the starting point. Select only the lenses relevant to the decision:

- **Users/customers:** needs, pain points, behaviour, willingness to adopt or pay, and unmet expectations.
- **Environment:** market, technology, platform, regulation, ecosystem, and relevant trends.
- **Alternatives/competition:** direct competitors, indirect substitutes, adjacent solutions, and the option of doing nothing.
- **Internal position:** skills, time, budget, distribution, assets, relationships, technical constraints, and operational capacity.
- **Current evidence:** traction, user feedback, usage, costs, reliability, and previous experiments.

Do not invent market size, demand, competitor capabilities, or user preferences. Research current external facts when they materially affect the decision. Separate verified evidence from estimates, hypotheses, and unknowns.

### 3. Identify the real strategic problem or opportunity

Describe the central obstacle or opportunity in a way that explains why a strategic choice is needed. Avoid jumping directly from a broad ambition to a list of features.

Check whether the apparent problem is actually:

- a user-demand problem;
- a differentiation or positioning problem;
- a distribution/reach problem;
- a capability or execution problem;
- a cost, sustainability, or business-model problem;
- a timing, platform, or dependency problem;
- a prioritisation problem caused by limited resources.

Do not assume every project needs a commercial business model. Match the diagnosis to the user's actual purpose, whether personal, creative, educational, community, open-source, or commercial.

### 4. Generate a small set of meaningfully different strategic choices

Develop a few distinct directions rather than cosmetic variations of the same idea. Depending on context, choices may differ by:

- target audience or use case;
- problem to solve first;
- positioning and differentiator;
- product scope or level of ambition;
- distribution or adoption path;
- build-versus-buy/partner approach;
- speed, quality, cost, or risk posture;
- sequence of capabilities to develop.

Include the status quo, a narrower option, or a decision to defer/stop when relevant. Do not force a large option list when the user needs a focused decision.

### 5. Test strategic coherence and trade-offs

For each serious option, consider:

- **Fit:** Does it address the diagnosed problem and the user's actual goals?
- **Differentiation:** Why would the intended audience choose or value it over alternatives?
- **Feasibility:** Can the available team, skills, time, money, and technology support it?
- **Viability:** Can it remain useful or sustainable over the relevant horizon? Apply commercial viability only when the user's goal requires it.
- **Capability fit:** What must be learned, built, acquired, or partnered for?
- **Opportunity cost:** What important work will not be done if this path is chosen?
- **Dependencies and fragility:** Which platforms, suppliers, APIs, people, or assumptions could undermine it?
- **Reversibility:** How costly is it to change direction later?
- **Risks and unintended effects:** What could make the strategy fail or create undesirable outcomes?

State the decisive trade-off for each option. Do not describe every option as simultaneously optimal.

### 6. Choose priorities and define what not to do

Recommend a direction only when the user's objectives and available evidence support it. Make the recommendation conditional when important facts remain unknown.

A useful strategy identifies:

- the primary focus;
- the reason this focus is preferable now;
- the capabilities/resources that must be concentrated;
- secondary opportunities to defer;
- explicit non-goals or exclusions;
- the conditions that would justify changing direction.

When evidence is insufficient, recommend a targeted test or information-gathering step instead of pretending the strategic choice is settled.

### 7. Define evidence, metrics, and review triggers

Translate the strategy into a small set of observable signals. Select measures that reflect the actual desired outcome, not vanity metrics.

Where useful, distinguish:

- **Outcome measures:** whether the intended result is happening.
- **Leading indicators:** early signals that may predict the outcome.
- **Guardrails:** quality, cost, safety, reliability, or user-experience limits that must not be sacrificed.
- **Learning measures:** what an experiment must reveal to reduce uncertainty.

Define a review point or trigger: what evidence would support continuing, expanding, narrowing, pivoting, or stopping? Avoid arbitrary precision or targets without a baseline or rationale.

### 8. Convert the chosen strategy into an execution plan

Only after the direction is sufficiently clear, use `planning-execution.md` to define milestones, dependencies, owners, work packages, acceptance criteria, and verification. Keep strategy decisions visible so execution does not silently drift from them.

## Scenario and uncertainty handling

For decisions exposed to significant uncertainty, consider a small number of plausible scenarios rather than a single confident forecast. Identify which assumptions are common to all scenarios and which could change the preferred option.

Prefer reversible experiments when uncertainty is high and the cost of learning is low. For expensive or difficult-to-reverse commitments, seek stronger evidence before recommending commitment.

## Strategy quality checks

Before presenting a strategic recommendation, check:

1. Is the strategic question explicit?
2. Is the diagnosis supported by evidence or clearly labelled assumptions?
3. Are the options genuinely different?
4. Does the recommendation explain both the choice and what is being sacrificed?
5. Are resource constraints and capabilities realistic?
6. Are market/competitor claims traceable to sources when research was needed?
7. Are success signals and review triggers actionable?
8. Is the next step proportional to the uncertainty and stakes?

Avoid generic SWOT tables, unsupported TAM/SAM/SOM figures, made-up competitor gaps, false certainty about future outcomes, and strategy jargon that does not change a decision.

## Proportionate output

- For a simple direction question, provide a short diagnosis, options, trade-off, and recommendation.
- For a substantial strategy request, structure the answer around context, strategic issue, choices, evaluation, recommended direction, non-goals, measures, risks, and next steps.
- Do not force a full strategy canvas or business plan when the user asks only for a quick strategic opinion.
- Do not claim a strategy is validated until relevant users, data, or experiments have tested its key assumptions.
