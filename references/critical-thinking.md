# Critical thinking and argument evaluation

## Purpose
Use when the user asks for critique, evaluation, reasoning, or analysis of a claim, proposal, explanation, or decision.

## Evaluate the argument, not the person

1. Identify the main claim or conclusion.
2. Identify the evidence offered and its source or quality.
3. Reconstruct the key inference connecting evidence to conclusion.
4. Surface load-bearing assumptions.
5. Check scope, definitions, causal reasoning, counterexamples, and alternative explanations.
6. Separate factual errors from value differences, preferences, and legitimate uncertainty.
7. Reach a conclusion proportionate to the evidence.

## Steelman before critique
Represent the strongest reasonable version of the user's argument before challenging it. Do not attack a weaker version that the user did not make.

## Anchor criticism
For supplied text, point to the specific statement or passage that creates the issue. For spoken or summarized ideas, restate the exact claim being assessed. Avoid vague comments such as “this is flawed” without showing why.

## Useful issue labels
Use sparingly and explain them in ordinary language:

- **Evidence gap:** an important claim lacks adequate support.
- **Inference gap:** the evidence does not establish the conclusion.
- **Hidden assumption:** the argument depends on an unstated premise.
- **Scope mismatch:** evidence from one population, time, or context is applied too broadly.
- **Internal conflict:** two parts of the argument cannot both be true as stated.
- **Alternative explanation:** another plausible cause or interpretation has not been considered.
- **Cannot assess:** required data, domain knowledge, or source access is missing.

Only name a formal logical fallacy when its definition clearly applies. Do not use fallacy labels as insults or substitutes for explanation.

## Proportionality
Use a quick assessment for ordinary discussion. Reserve a full argument map or detailed rubric for important decisions, proposals, documents, or explicit requests for deep review. Focus on the few issues that matter most.

## Response shape
When useful, structure the result as: strongest case → key weaknesses → what evidence would resolve them → balanced conclusion or next test.
