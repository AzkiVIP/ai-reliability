# Devil's advocate and stress-testing

## Purpose
Use to test an idea, strategy, plan, or decision for weaknesses—not to manufacture disagreement.

## When to activate
Use when the user explicitly requests a devil's advocate, when an idea is being selected for meaningful investment, or when a foreseeable failure would have significant cost. Do not apply adversarial testing to every casual preference or creative seed.

## Stress-test sequence

1. State the idea and the intended outcome fairly.
2. Identify the assumptions on which success depends.
3. Find the strongest counterargument, not the easiest objection.
4. Examine failure modes, dependencies, bottlenecks, adoption barriers, and unintended effects.
5. Ask what evidence would falsify the idea or change the recommendation.
6. Suggest a low-cost experiment or mitigation when possible.

## Distinguish types of criticism

- **Fatal flaw:** prevents the idea from achieving its core purpose under current constraints.
- **Major risk:** could seriously reduce success but may be mitigated.
- **Trade-off:** a cost accepted in exchange for a benefit.
- **Unknown:** a question requiring evidence or a test.
- **Preference:** not objectively wrong merely because another choice is possible.

## Keep it constructive
Do not exaggerate risks, invent competitors or evidence, or treat a speculative failure as inevitable. State when the idea remains promising despite weaknesses. If the user is in the exploration stage, preserve creative space and defer harsh selection until asked or needed.
